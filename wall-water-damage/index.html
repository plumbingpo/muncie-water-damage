Wall and Drywall Water Damage: What Muncie Homeowners Should Know

Think of drywall as a wick. Once the base of a wall gets wet, moisture climbs through the paper and gypsum, commonly a foot or more higher than the water stood on the floor. Insulation and the rear of the panel can be wet through while the paint out front shows nothing unusual. That is why we check walls with a moisture meter instead of trusting how they look.

Not every wet wall has to be torn out. Firm panels wetted by clean water are frequently saved, in some cases with the baseboard taken off and small openings drilled so air can move inside the wall. When the wall is soft, crumbling, moldy or was soaked by dirty water, we make a flood cut, a straight line above the damage, and remove everything below it.

What It Looks Like

  • Bubbling, wrinkled or peeling paint and wallpaper low on the wall
  • Baseboards that swell, warp or pull away from the wall
  • Discolored lower drywall with a visible line marking how high water wicked
  • One wall noticeably cooler under your hand than the rest
  • Wet tack strip, lifting planks or stained flooring at the base of the wall
  • Speckled growth showing up where furniture sits against the wall or at corners
What We Do

How We Handle Wall and Drywall Water Damage

1

Find Every Wet Area

We scan each wall with a moisture meter and thermal camera and mark how high and how far the water traveled, including the opposite side and adjoining rooms.

2

Sort Save From Remove

Based on the water category, how long it sat and how the drywall feels, we tell you plainly which sections can be dried in place and which need to come out.

3

Open Up or Air Out

Walls we are keeping have their baseboards removed so air can be pushed into the cavity. Walls that cannot be kept get a flood cut along a level line, with the wet insulation pulled.

4

Drying and Reconstruction

Dehumidifiers and air movers stay on, usually 3 to 5 days, until the studs and the drywall left in place test dry. After that comes insulation, new drywall hung and finished, trim put back and paint.

Behind the Surface

The Damage You Do Not See

Soaked Batts in the Wall

Once wet, fiberglass insulation stays wet and keeps the studs damp. Drying from inside the room is not enough when the insulation is soaked.

Wet Sheathing Outside

Outside walls have sheathing past the insulation that can also get soaked. Because it is slow to dry, we test it before any new drywall goes up.

Electrical Hazards Low Down

Water pools at the same height as most receptacles and cable runs. Leave the outlets on a damp wall alone until someone has checked the circuit.

Walls You Cannot See

Sections of wall covered by cabinets, a vanity or shelving stay damp because air never reaches them. Checking under the cabinet base and on the reverse face tells us if it is wet.

Why Speed Matters

What Happens if You Wait

The first few hours

Water Starts Climbing

The bottom edge of the drywall takes in water from the floor and it begins moving up. Baseboards and the bottom plate take on moisture.

Hours 24 to 48

Swelling and Blisters

Moisture keeps climbing, the baseboards puff up and blisters form in the paint. Mold can start on the paper facing inside the cavity.

Day 3 to 7

Panels Lose Strength

Paper peels from a core that is no longer firm. Within the wall the insulation sags, a smell develops and mold moves over the back of the panel.

Past one week

Damage Reaches the Studs

Studs and plates stay wet long enough to grow mold and begin to decay. Much more wall now has to be removed and rebuilt.
